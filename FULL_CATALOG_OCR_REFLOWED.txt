Traveling Exhibition

36 Contemporary Artists who. are associated with twelve Latin American countries will take part in an 18 month tour of university galleries and museums which has been provided by the generosity of the Elizabeth Firestone Graham Foundation. According to Carol Norman, the curator, “this show is a sampling of the work of thirty-six Latin American artists. It is a collection of individual voices put together in an effort to present at least a small taste of the incrediblie abundance of contemporary Latin American art that is currently being produced across the Americas.”

This traveling exhibition was organized by East Tennessee State University Department of Art & Design, Johnson City, Tennessee.

forward

This exhibition is a collection of the art of thirty-six individuals, each producing a compelling body of work and each having a substantial link to Latin America. The work was gathered with the intent to share the art of contemporary Latin Americans with people in the United States. The show does not pretend to be an all-inclusive survey of contemporary Latin American art, but is presented as a taste of its incredible diversity.

Within most of the U.S. art curricula, both historic and contemporary Latin American art have been ignored. Beyond a certain familiarity with the Mexican muralists, and a few others like Frieda Khalo, Matta, and Botero, even many art professionals are unfamiliar with it. Seeking to make up for this deficit in my own studies, I began investigating Latin American art and was impressed by the diversity, vitality, formal strength and personal vision of much of the work.

Latin American art is highly visible in Miami and may always be found in New York, but unfortunately, there are many people in this country who are still unable to see such art without traveling to a large city. This touring exhibition was conceived as a means of sharing the experience of this art with as many interested people of the United States as possible.

We use “Latin American Artists” as a broad term, no more descriptive of the particular than “European Artists” or “North American Artists.” We include artists who currently live in Latin America, others who are originally from Central and South America but reside in the United States; and artists who were born and/or raised here but maintain close identification with their ancestral homes.

Rather than select a particular theme or representative look, we have collected a varied body of work without a significant visual common denominator. We find however, that the term “Latin” does conjure up specific and stereotyped images in many of our minds. Thus, out of the abundance of powerful Latin American art being produced today, we have brought this small sample in the hope of dispelling some of these limiting conceptions and increasing the opportunities for our direct experience of contemporary art.

It has been a thrilling experience to curate and assemble this show. We are pleased to be able to present this work, loaned to us by the participating artists, who have so graciously shared their art and their experience in order to enrich ours. We hope you enjoy it.

Julio Antonio

Julio Antonio has been in this country since 1986. He arrived as a political refuge and, in many senses, is still running from the repressive regime of Fidel Castro’s Cuba. His work centers around the lies of Castro and reflects the great loss and fears of the Cuban people. The distorted profile of Castro often leers from his work. The face of Castro, as an inescapable reality and an unshakable presence, appears often in Antonio’s work, as does the form of the black hand. For a people oppressed on all sides, who never know exactly who or where the agents of power may lurk, the image of the black hand represents invisible powers at work and connotes certain and crippling fear.

In Ironic Bubble, a face which is a metaphor for Castro (although Antonio remarks that it looks more like himself than Fidel), appears to speak in a manner which is both innocent and philosophically correct : “I only know that I don’t know nothing.” The face of one of the dead responds in Spanish “Si pero yo sé que me jodiste,” which translates, “Yes, but I know that you screwed me.” Castro is the obvious reference for the first individual, but it could allude to anyone who refuses to take personal responsibility and maintains the guise of innocence while trampling on the lives of others around them.

In The Last Rafter, an automobile tire serves as a makeshift boat and encircles the image of a person, perhaps a child, more dead than alive. The figure depicts one of those many Cubans who found life unbearable on the island and risked death by sharks or drowning in the hope of reaching freedom in small boats and makeshift rafts. Of those that survived, few found freedom as we know it.

By naming the painting The Last Rafter, Antonio expresses his hope that Cubans will be able to survive without having to take such desperate risks. The placement of the black hand within the head indicates simultaneously that the person in Antonio's tire has been defeated by the secret powers, and that he has incorporated the black hand into his life. It has been imprinted on his mind and burned into his experience, so that even if he were one of the survivors, he would carry the prison of fear within him.

Maria Brito 

Originally from Cuba, Maria Brito came to this country when she was thirteen years old and received her education here. She is a woman who balanced the multiple roles of wife, mother, artist and teacher while finding her own means of expression. She speaks clearly and provides us the opportunity to communicate directly with her art.

In Old Cabinet with Jar, the remains of a forgotten and graying medicine cabinet hang on the wall. It has the appearance of something which has survived the destruction of a home. Small blocks, each with the image of an eye, a nose, or lips, rest inside the cabinet. Like abandoned looks, each for certain situations, they suggest the bits of a false face we all present at times.

Because of the nature of this show, we are only able to include small pieces of her work but I feel that we should mention that Maria Brito creates installations, rooms you could walk into, slices of life. Her memories become ours, some painful, some joyous, but all immediate. Communicating visually, viscerally, and beyond words, her work speaks in our private languages. Brito seems to be always ready to present that raw essence of experience we all know to be true. Her painted images seem direct rather than symbolic. In them she offers raw, almost confrontational images without concern for veneer.

Although Maria Brito does not think of herself as a painter, her painting is extraordinarily powerful. San Rémy is a painting on a conventional canvas, with a nine-foot plank leaning up against it. The painting continues to the plank, allowing the work to protrude into our space while bringing to mind the image of the cross. A brightly painted figure lurches forward with his tongue hanging out as he reaches for a glass of water which sits on an ornate ledge attached to the painting. Both the title and the image refer to Van Gogh’s stay at the sanitarium at San Rémy. The whole structure of the work reminds us of Van Gogh’s passionate struggle to survive by finding expression in art; of his instability, and of his attempts to gain control of his vision through paint. We immediately recognize that this painting is more than a convenient art historical reference. It is nothing less than the pain of existence which explodes out of the gray and refuses to be contained by the flat surface of a canvas.

Alicia Candiani

Alicia Candiani lives in Buenos Aires where she works and teaches. She uses Renaissance images to confront social and political issues. Working in series, she often spends years investigating and developing each theme fully. Recently she has dealt with issues of political violence in Latin America, the environment and gender bias.

For her Feminine Gender series, Candiani appropriated images from art history, scanned, distorted, and output them digitally, then xerox- transferred the images onto lithographic plates which she printed in a


=========================================
  PAGE 07  [page07Master_TXT_OCR.txt]
=========================================


conventional manner. Working with chine collé, she has introduced intensely colored Japanese papers into her print, so that it has the quality of a stained glass window.

Her appropriation is done for conceptual reasons rather than for convenience. It is not used merely to incorporate an art historical reference, but to pass judgment on that history itself by bringing attention to the male viewpoint from which it was created and is presented. In an attempt to right the wrongs of history by redoing it, Candiani places feminine characteristics onto male bodies.

Myself in a Male Body is based on Raphael’s portrait of the patron Angelo Doni, which Candiani has distorted, as women’s image of themselves has been distorted by the viewpoints of a male dominated society. As though she were trying on a role for size, Candiani has added the sewn-on mask of own her face, and a breastplate (with breasts) laced up like a corset onto the original image. The figure is placed in front of a Renaissance building. Female nudes are positioned along the top of the building like Renaissance sculptures. Like images from Muybridge’s or Marey’s early movement studies, they gesture and turn before us, further pointing to the male perception of the female figure as an object of desire.

Do Not Speak, Do Not Argue features an image, again based on a Renaissance work, of a woman with her mouth sewn shut. This figure addresses the expected role of women within a patriarchal society. Candiani refers to a male domination which is both brutal and subtle. As a resident of Buenos Aires, a city where women are very visible in the work force, Candiani refers to a pattern of discrimination which functions principally as a glass ceiling beyond which women cannot rise.

While Alicia Candiani makes a point of relating her work to issues relevant to contemporary life, the formal visual beauty of this art provides a visual lushness equal to the strength of its content .

Tony Capellán

In Pequena Esperanza, (Small Hope) Tony Capellán uses very accessible and simple means to address issues of deep human concern. Forty-two little shirts, sized for small children and dyed in various greens and browns, hang on lines of sisal twine. Each shirt has in its little pocket a bakery roll or a pencil. The message is dual: it is a “small hope,” in the sense that it is not too much to ask for, to be able to provide for their children’s basic needs for food, clothing and education. But there also is “little hope” that this will be so. There are many people for whom education and nourishment are impossibilities. Capellán probably intends a more specific interpretation and may be directly addressing specific

=========================================


social issues within the Dominican Republic. Yet, he presents a view so basic that it is impossible not to extend the thought to all of mankind. This piece, in its directness and simplicity, is one of the most eloquent I have ever seen.

Elizabeth Cerejido 

[Elizabeth Cerejido | Nude XII | rawMasters_Corrected/page08Master_ART.tif#left]
[Elizabeth Cerejido | Nude LXII | rawMasters_Corrected/page08Master_ART.tif#right]

Originally from Cuba, Elizabeth Cerejido grew up in Miami where she continues to live and work. In her Nude series, she uses the figure in a way which is more formal than emotive. Even though the work evokes a sense of mystery, we are rarely tempted to think in terms of a narrative or to understand the relationship between the figures in human terms. The focus is compositional. The form of the body, the variety of movement, the surface of the skin and the range of tonalities it yields all become the tools of Cerejido’s art. She spends considerable time in the darkroom manipulating the subtle relationship between tones and creating the resonant depth of the darkness in her work.

In the earlier pieces in the series like Nude XII she investigates the solidity of form. Her figures emerge out of a blanket of darkness thereby creating an ambiguity between figure and ground. Parts of the bodies remain invisible which makes the darkness seem as tangible as a solid form from which a figure emerges, like one of Michelangelo’s slaves. In the latter pieces of the series Cerejido has changed her focus.

In Nude LXII the velvet blackness of the background is still present but now movement becomes primary. Even though we are viewing a static scene, we are aware of the transitory nature of the pose. The very placement of the figures and the diagonals formed by the repetition of limbs create the illusion of dance.

Karina Chechik

The Last Dialogue is based on text from Rayuela (Hopscotch) by Julio Cortázar, the acclaimed Argentinean writer. The passage on which she focuses is about the love relationship of two people and the spaces between them. Incorporating the actual text as an element in her paintings, Chechik creates a visual fabric woven together of fragments of experience. Basing her images on photographs which she took of scenes from her own life, we are presented with a kaleidoscopic view of a human relationship. Tea cups on a table, his foot, a ring, her foot, a drain, threads of a narrative not fully told, and words, the text of the story of the chapter of Rayuela she is using. Here, the use of the text, as a relevant and meaningful source also brings to mind the interplay of words in a relationship, the attempts to bridge the spaces between, not only to communicate, but also to clutter the silence of our separateness.

The literary is often an element in Chechik’s art. She is well known for her Labyrinth series, based on the Jorge Luis Borges work of the same name, in which she deals with the existential currents of human experience. These dark and mysterious paintings create the sense of vast passages filled with mist. Although the photo images she uses are based on subjects as concrete as the architecture of Buenos Aires and New York, we experience the sensation of something shifting, insubstantial, and not quite real.

The more recent Rayuela pieces are in a lighter mode. The color, while still very subtle, plays in a higher key and her choice of earthen tints sets this series clearly in the corporeal world. The images, though tilted and fragmentary, are intimate glimpses of everyday life. The Last Dialogue triptych from this series is made up of three vertical, glass front boxes after the manner of Louis Cornell.

The Rayuela series, made up of multiple small canvases reminiscent of an actual hopscotch game, makes us aware of the passage of time. The small scale and repetitive nature of the presentation which appear as stills from a movie or fragments of memory which we play over in our minds creates an obsessive quality . The fragmentary images appear like something we chase, a space not bridged, or the illusive relationship, sought after but never quite fulfilled. The text confirms this, but if we are unable to decipher the words, the painting, images and format, speak clearly enough. Partly because of the triune nature of the piece itself and partly because of the row of large votive candles placed centrally on the floor of the middle box, the piece has an altar-like quality and presents the idea of seeking assistance from sources beyond, a lighting of a candle for aid in the realm of love.

Esperanza Cortés

Esperanza Cortés, originally from Columbia, has lived in New York for the last twenty years. In her work two- and three-dimensional forms merge in a way which seem to incorporate the physical world with a spiritual dimension beyond. Her imagery does the same; the body and the tree become one, both physical realities, but their combination casts them into an altered dimension. Many of her figures make reference to birth, literally to childbirth, but Cortés also creates an awareness of the process of growth, of becoming, or blossoming.

She uses both encaustic and fresco, sometimes uniting the two. Her figures, sculpted in clay and adhered centrally onto a plaster covered arch have the appearance of icons. The immobile frontal figures, reminiscent of the artistic conventions of earlier cultures, creates the illusion of timelessness and further supports the idea of the image as an icon. The network of veins, stems and leaves, delicately painted on both the figures and the frescoed surface behind enhance this sense of continuity and growth

In Alas Rosadas (Rose-colored Wings), Cortés depicts a baby in the womb of a woman who has rose-colored wings. Beautifully formed vein and branch structures sprout and spread throughout her body. The wings suggest the woman’s hopes, prayers, and her desire to protect the infant who grows within her. At the same time she is transformed, taken over by this growth and she merged with the tree and a life-giving relationship that allows them to bloom.

In Tree of Sustenance, we are again presented with the female experiences of birth, nursing, nurturing, which again seem to be combined with the idea of cosmic growth or an unfolding of a spiritual substance. In both of these pieces Cortés presents the embodiment of the female principle, simultaneously indicating development and continuity. In the process of clarifying her own identity she merges forms and ideas from the pre-Columbian world into a contemporary consciousness.

Teresa & Allen Diehl

Teresa Diehl was born in Lebanon, raised in Venezuela, and currently lives in Miami with her husband Allen. Partners in everything, they take photographs together so that the work is the result of an ongoing collaboration and at times they are not even sure who took which photos.

Teresa Diehl is a masterful professional photographer who often uses her skills to accurately photograph the work of other artists. This is an exacting procedure which demands a high level of technical expertise. I mention this fact only because it highlights the dynamic of her approach to her art.

In her personal work, Ms. Diehl throws away this element of control to interact with the somewhat haphazard, but always spontaneous, results of a Polaroid camera. The Polaroid is not the tool to use for accurate color but it does allow for a dialog between the creator and the work, similar to the way in which a painter can experience the immediacy of the brush stroke and respond to it. Not only does she exchange rational control for intuitive response, but she incorporates randomness into the mix. Her subjects are the things of everyday life. She presents us with momentary glimpses of the people and things around her, at times as out of focus as life itself.

Often her images catch movement or emotion, sometimes they function more as an abstract arrangement of shapes as in the untitled work from the series... from Allen and Teresa. We are pulled into this dynamic composition by the strong repeated diagonals of arms placed across the surface of the print. Yet, we are not quite sure what we are seeing and must stop and look in an attempt to sort and rationalize the image. The photo functions as an unexplained narrative. It becomes a clue, whose meaning changes according to, the way in which we perceive it, or the images placed beside it . I am reminded that Marcel Duchamp spoke of a “reciprocal coefficient,” that portion of a work of art that is completed in the mind of the viewer. It is in this way that Teresa and Allen Diehl’s work actively invites our participation.

Edouard Duval-Carrié

Edouard Duval-Carrié is both painter and sculptor. A native of Haiti who is currently working in Miami. His bronze sculptures, which we have included in this show, are part of a larger body of work, The Vaudou Parthenum, in which he has created and cast images for thirty of the more than one thousand deities common to the Vaudou religion.

The bronzes are appropriately reminiscent of the Yoruba sculptures from the eleventh century. When the people native to the Caribbean were decimated by the Spanish sword and European disease, they were replaced by African slaves, who were imported to work the sugar plantations. Their origin was often in Nigeria where the victors of area wars would sell the losers into slavery. The slaves brought the cultural traditions of Nigeria with them and managed to preserve them in the new world. Duval-Carrié takes his format for his bronzes more from the tradition of the Ife and Benin bronzes than from any Greco-Roman model

In much of the Caribbean, where the culture is a mix of European, African and some Native American traditions, the personages of the


=========================================
  PAGE 15  [page15Master_TXT_OCR.txt]
=========================================


African religions are an important influence on daily life and the choices made therein. While these deities are still worshipped today in many areas of the Americas, they are usually not presented in bronze. Many of them have been merged with the saints of Catholicism and appear like St. Barbara or St. Lazarus, having the face of a traditional Catholic saint, but imbued with the personality and powers of the African deity. This is true for both Vaudou and Santeria, Also images, or signs which refer to them are often prepared for a ceremony and then destroyed afterwards. By creating a bronze pantheon of the Vaudou gods, Duval-Carrié, seeks to legitimize them by affording them the same treatment the Greek and Roman pantheon received. Yet. it is not an easy job to put faces on unseen forces and although his images are based on his own research and understanding of the personalities and attributes of these deities, they are also a fanciful and creative work which can be appreciated on a purely formal basis.

In Le Grand Bois Des Islet, (The Great Woods of the Island), the face of the deity is incorporated into that of a tree. The pegs which are inserted into the eyes and throughout the piece are placed there as a reference to the manner of summoning this deity by hammering on wood.

Felipe Ehrenberg

Felipe Ehrenberg. a painter, printmaker, maker of books, and installations, and performance artist has explored and developed many facets of art. He has made a significant impact on the production of artist books in several areas of the world. In his native Mexico where he is currently living, he has been an important force in establishing the contemporary small press movement which encourages artists to create and print small editions of experimental artist books.

Mexican art has often been coupled with a national political activism in which the art becomes a vehicle for the expression of populist sentiments. During the decade after their revolution, the graphic movements were particularly important in spreading political awareness among the people through the use of art. Therefore, the very choice of the print as a medium makes a visual connection to the post revolutionary era of Mexican Expressionism.

The Ehrenberg pieces we have included in this show are silk screened images based on a few of the installations which he has created. Primero de Enero - I (January the First - 1) is Ehrenberg’s response to the “Mayan Wars” race wars which began on January 1, 1994 in Chiapas which by now have spread to the rest of the Mexico. Eugenio Espinosa

Tumba, Manglar y Puente (Tomb, Mangrove And Bridge) is a constructed painting which combines the shape of a bridge and an arch which can suggest the shape of a tombstone or a tomb or a rock cut tomb perhaps with the stone rolled away. We do get the sense of resurrection from this work with its leaves growing victoriously out of the tangled mass of gray roots which engulf the bridge of the lower half of the piece. There is something triumphant in the intense deep red and golden green coloration of the upper part. The mangrove grows, in a swamp, where decaying matter provides the nutrients to produce new life.

When l asked Espinosa about the source of this piece he spoke of the attempt to capture memories, fleeting images of a childhood on an island, in an almost forgotten time. It is a difficult job, to bring to life a memory, to give it form while still allowing it to breathe. This painting then, records the act of its creation. In struggling with the form and the image, the artists wrestles with himself and the work, until a resolution brings forth an alive and complete image .

Espinosa often incorporates a sculptural element into his work, whether just in the construction of his shaped surfaces or in actually carving parts of the piece as in Calavera where he carves into and cuts through the polystyrene structure to create cavities reminiscent of a skull or a heart.

Eduardo Daniel Fiorda

The first thing I saw when I walked into Eduardo Daniel Fiorda’s studio was a large bird-like creature made out of the springs and mechanical assembly of an old convertible couch. Looking fully capable of flight, it reminded me of the robotic skeletons of the fantastic monsters of the movies.

Bits of electronics, used spark plugs, and scrap metal are just a few of the materials which Fiorda uses for his sculpture. By recycling these discarded items, he transforms them into art. Although this idea is not


=========================================
  PAGE 19  [page19Master_TXT_OCR.txt]
=========================================


new, it does highlight an important aspect of our lifestyle. Much of our time is spent in obtaining material goods, wearing them out and then discarding them when they break. Ours is a replacement society, in which it is often more economical to just buy something new rather than to attempt to fix a broken item. Artists such as Fiorda use this vast supply of materials to make a type of art that is not only ingenious but accessible. Part of my delight with his hulk of a bird was that I could simultaneously see the aesthetics of the form and imagine the couch that birthed it.

The reasoning for this choice of materials is manifold. It makes a comment on the tremendous waste of our society and goes against the tide of constant consumerism. Also, it seeks to take away the preciousness of art and place it on a more pedestrian level.

In a manner somewhat reminiscent of a cross between “Arte Povera’ and Sesame Street, Fiorda’s creatures all exude strong personal character. The pieces we have included in this show are from a series he did based on toys. Heavy Toys, the helicopter family, composed of five distinct small pieces, has that wonderful, whimsical quality that so much of his work exhibits and also the economy of form that makes each little copter a delight.

Florencio Gelabert

Florencio Gelabert is one of the latest generation of Cuban artists to come to this country. He currently lives in Miami. His pieces which we have included in this show are from a larger body of work dealing with the attempt to transcend violence, abandon anger and function on a higher plane. He composes the pieces out of rusted weaponry and burned implements. The weaponry, crafted out of agricultural or nautical tools, implies the physicality of struggle; the fight for a vision. The mace and flail-like structure of the weaponry reminds us of the brutality of the conquistadors and the history of the continuing struggles in Latin America. Yet in the end, the idea of transcendence does speak forth because the implements themselves have been converted into art work.

Maria Gnecco 

Maria Gnecco spent several years in Brazil and became interested in the Afro-American religious influences and practices prevalent there .Taking her inspiration from the Yoruba religions as they are practiced in Brazil and Cuba, she chose animal forms based on the traditions of their deities as her subject matter. The bird, “fallen from the hand of Obatala,” and the fish, symbolic of Yemaya, are persistent themes in her work.

Luz Maria Gordillo

In the Fallen Angels series, Luz Gordillo created a series of photographs which marked a new direction for her work. Although she has always sought to address the concerns of women and based her work on the female body, in the Fallen Angels series, she subverts the role of the documentary photographer as impartial observer by participating in them. Gordillo herself is the focus, thereby transforming the work from photojournalism to the documentation of performance art.

Her desire is to make a social and political statement about the oppression of women in Latin America. Pablo Soria

Pablo Soria is interested in history. He was born in Tucumán, Argentina in an area of the Andean mountains rich in historic sites and artifacts, both colonial and pre-Columbian. Miguel Trelles

Miguel Trelles is a young printmaker and painter who comes from Puerto Rico and maintains galleries both there and in New York. The pieces we have included in this show are from a series of lithographs he made when he traveled to Paris as an art student. The series is called Tainos in Parts referring to himself as Taino, but at least in part inspired by the pre-Columbian art he saw exhibited in Paris.

The Taínos were the people occupying Puerto Rico when it was conquered by the Spanish. Through disease and cruel treatment. their numbers dwindled and now they are represented only by their artifacts. Trelles incorporates the Taino figure into these prints as a symbol for his own identity as a Caribbean islander. Autobiographical in nature, these pieces include combined elements of Paris and the artwork of the Louvre along with the Chinese calligraphy that Trelles was studying at the time. The characters in Tainos in Paris: Louver say “I love you,” expressing Trelles” appreciation of the art and culture of Paris, while in Tainos in Paris: Boriquén they read “you do not love me” recording the coldness of that city’s reception of him.

Jorge Vera

Both of the photographic images we have from Jorge Vera show a flair for the formal. Their textures sparkle crisply, revealing the linear and tonal patterns within the work.

Born and raised in Lima, Peru, Vera came to this country when he was twenty-one. He trained in printmaking and sculpture in Peru, but chose to study photography when he arrived in the U.S. After he came to this country, he lived in an area of Missouri which had no real Latin American community and he felt isolated by the cultural difference. This separation was reflected in his work by his treatment of human subjects. Depicting people from a distance or from behind, as objects, which are arranged for their composition rather than as individuals, he never offered the possibility of communion.

Later pieces, like Day at the Beach, have a more personal nature and reflect a fuller interaction with the subject. This piece, which documents a happy event in the artist’s life, was taken on the day he was reunited with his children after an extended separation. While the formal qualities are still striking, there is now a human warmth. We move up over the sculpted textural surface to the warmth and delight of a child’s face.

afterword

By the end of the second half of the twentieth century we have already deconstructed most coherent systems of thought into fragmentary ideas separated from a cultural matrix. The artistic process becomes one of reconstructing innumerable and individual threads of thought and experience into a coherent whole. Each artist speaks from the specific uniqueness of an individual existence and melds their personal, familial and cultural inclinations and experiences into a distinct voice. At the end of a century of technological revolution we can now write notes around the globe and immediately access information from remote locations. Our lifestyles have become eclectic. We pick and choose our philosophies. Moreover, contemporary art is trans cultural in nature and composed of many individual voices, functioning as the harmonics of an experience that is essentially human.

In the Americas, this century has been a time for stepping away from Europeanism and establishing not only political independence, but also independent cultural centers. The development of individual artists as well as workshops and art movements reflects, with their various strains of African and European and indigenous influences, the eclectic nature of the new world. At the turn of the twenty-first century major urban cultural centers on both American continents thrive and foster the production of much valuable work.

If the premise that art enriches the lives of its participants is valid (and I believe it is), we ought to increase the opportunities for its production and circulation, both here and abroad. The exchange of art and ideas about art between the Americas is valuable and important, particularly since there is currently a certain deficit of information in the U.S. The interchange of art and artists between the Americas is fairly easy to facilitate and can only be beneficial to all the recipients.

However, at least in this country, an artificial gap exists between the artists and the people. The art world makes art inaccessible and perpetuates the opposition of an art elite to the mainstream of society. Critics often write only as a gesture to others in the field. Why not establish a critical mode that can help integrate art into our daily lives instead of constructing wordy briar patches around it?
